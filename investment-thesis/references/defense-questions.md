# Defense Questions — Prepared Answers

This file is for use when the user pushes back on a verdict, asks "are you sure?", or is preparing for academic/jury defense (e.g., MBA classes, pitch competitions, LP meetings). Read it before answering hostile follow-ups.

The thesis is built to survive these. The answers below are templates — adapt the specifics to the deal at hand, but keep the structure and the stance.

---

## The "Grumpy LP" attack: gate vs. moat coherence

**Attack:** "Your filter sounds crisp but hides a contradiction: you say buyer-side reality is the gating question, *not* the technology, yet you also claim 'no moat, no money,' which can smuggle technology back in. State your decision rule with no startup-specific language: what makes buyer-side reality the gating question, and what non-technology moat is sufficient to say yes?"

**Answer:** The rule has two steps applied in order, and they answer different questions.

**Step 1 — gate** asks: *can this product be sold?* It's buyer-side because it's about how the buyer experiences the problem. Three conditions: operational pain (a P&L line item the buyer can name today), single-signer buyer (one person with discretionary spend can sign), asymmetric switching cost (staying with the status quo hurts more than adopting). Technology is irrelevant here — it doesn't matter how clever the product is if no buyer with budget can sign.

**Step 2 — moat** asks: *can this product compound?* Only after the gate clears. Four kinds of moat count, and technology is not one of them: distribution moat (proprietary buyer access), workflow-locked data moat (state that can't be ported), buyer-side network effects (better with each new buyer), regulatory or trust moat (18+ months to replicate).

**Why technology is excluded from the moat list:** anything one team builds, another builds 12 months later for half the cost. Models get distilled. Algorithms get copied. Technology is a head start, not a moat. It's often present in the deals I do, but it's never the *reason* I do them.

**No contradiction** — the gate and moat answer different questions and both must clear independently. Gate first, moat second.

## "What does a yes look like?"

**Attack:** "Easy to say no to everything. What's a concrete profile of a deal you would say yes to?"

**Answer:** A "yes" deal under this thesis looks like:

- **Buyer**: European industrial SMB, 30–500M€ revenue. Operations director, plant manager, or finance director with €50–500k discretionary spend.
- **Pain**: a quantified % of a P&L line item the buyer owns — typically energy cost, downtime, working capital tied up in receivables, compliance reporting hours.
- **Sale**: legible to one signer, no committee. Pilot in 2–4 weeks. Go/no-go decision within 90 days.
- **Switching cost from status quo**: integration under one week, no data migration nightmare, no retraining of more than one team.
- **Moat**: at least one of the four — typically (a) vertical-specific data that compounds (e.g., predictive maintenance dataset specific to one machinery type), or (b) distribution channel I can open through my FR/DE/CH industrial network.
- **Stage**: pre-seed to seed. At least one founder full-time. Unit economics legible by Series A — i.e., CAC and LTV directionally provable from the first 10 customers.
- **Geography**: France, Germany, Switzerland, or transatlantic with European anchor.

That profile clears the gate (operational pain, single-signer, asymmetric switching) and the moat (vertical data or distribution leverage). It also clears co-founder fit because my network actually moves the needle.

Few deals match. That's the point — the filter exists to make passes principled and yeses meaningful.

## "Pre-seed companies pivot. Why kill it now?"

**Answer:** I'm not killing the company — I'm declining to fund this *configuration* today. Pricing, GTM, and team commitment all reflect strategy. If those are wrong now, they should be resolved *before* pre-seed money goes in, not after. Pre-seed dollars don't fix strategic confusion; they accelerate it. If the team resolves the issue, the conditions to revisit say what to look for.

---

## "TAM is huge. Why dismiss it?"

**Answer:** TAM is irrelevant if the product cannot capture it economically. The thesis is gate-then-moat, not TAM-then-vibes. A $200B market that the product cannot reach, or where the unit economics don't pencil at scale, contributes zero. I refuse the TAM-as-justification move on principle.

---

## "Many great companies started part-time. Why disqualify here?"

**Answer:** The disqualifier isn't "has ever been part-time" — it's "pre-seed raise with zero full-time commitment in a saturated category." If founders aren't sure enough to take the personal risk, why should an investor? Stripe, GitHub, Mailchimp all had at least one founder go full-time within months of incorporation, before raising. Part-time at pre-seed in a crowded market is a structural signal, not a stylistic one.

---

## "You met the team. Isn't that bias?"

**Answer:** The opposite. Direct meetings give me more information than a cold evaluator, not less — read on team capability, strategic thinking, product vision. The pass is *informed*, not reflexive. I'd update positively if I had reasons to. I don't, despite knowing them well enough to want to.

---

## "What would change your mind?"

**Answer:** This is the single most important question to have a clean answer for. The conditions to revisit in the evaluation should already cover this. If they don't, name three specific, measurable signals — typically: (1) commitment proof (full-time founder, raise closed from a credible lead), (2) traction proof (named cohort and velocity, e.g., 5+ paying customers per month sustained over a quarter), (3) unit-economics proof (CAC and LTV directionally provable, or a pricing/motion change that resolves the broken economics).

---

## "You're looking to join as co-founder. Why didn't you join this team?"

**Answer:** Co-founder fit requires both directions — my contribution must compound the venture, and the venture must serve my domain. Most deals I evaluate fail one direction or the other, often the same direction that fails the investment thesis. When I pass on a deal as investor, I almost always also pass as co-founder, for the same reasons.

## Three verbal anchors to hold under pressure

When the LP pushes hard, these phrases keep the answer on-thesis:

1. **"Pricing reflects strategy."** — Reframes any pricing question as a strategy question.
2. **"Both gate and moat must clear independently."** — Resolves any gate-vs-moat coherence attack.
3. **"TAM is irrelevant if the product cannot capture it economically."** — Refuses TAM-as-bait.

---

## What NOT to do in defense

- **Don't soften the verdict.** "I would neither fund nor join. Yes, I'm sure." Repeat as needed.
- **Don't add new arguments mid-defense.** Stay anchored to what's in the original evaluation. New claims = new attack surface.
- **Don't apologize for being negative.** A clear pass is more valuable than a fuzzy yes.
- **Don't disclaim "I might be wrong."** Of course you might be wrong — that's true of every thesis. Saying it weakens you.
