## Class Prep: Macroeconomics — Monetary Policy & the Fed

### Previous Session Summary

Last session covered how central banks set rates using systematic frameworks, with the 2022–2024 Fed hiking cycle as the primary case.

**The Taylor Rule** (John Taylor, 1993): `i = r* + π + 0.5(π - π*) + 0.5(y - y*)`. The nominal rate should equal the neutral real rate plus current inflation, adjusted for the gap between actual and target inflation, and the gap between actual and potential output. More inflation above target → higher prescribed rate. More slack in the economy → lower prescribed rate.

**The 2021 miss:** The Taylor Rule was prescribing rates above 4% by mid-2021. The Fed held near zero, calling inflation "transitory." This was a forecast error on the inputs, not a flaw in the rule itself — the Fed misjudged whether the inflation was durable.

**The 2022–2024 cycle:** 525 basis points of hikes in 16 months — fastest since Volcker. CPI peaked at 9.1% in June 2022. Terminal rate: 5.25–5.50% by July 2023. Held through mid-2024, then cuts began. By end-2024: 4.25–4.50%.

**The near-soft landing:** Inflation fell from 9.1% to ~3% without a recession. Unemployment rose only from 3.4% to ~4.1%. Historically rare. Whether this was skilled policy or self-healing supply chains remains genuinely contested.

**Transmission mechanism:** Higher FFR → higher borrowing costs → reduced spending and investment → lower aggregate demand → lower inflation. Lag of 12–24 months, which is why the Fed must act preemptively.

2. **Tariff Pass-Through Above 50% Creates Stagflation Risk** — *St. Louis Fed / Wellington Management, April 2026*
   The effective US tariff rate jumped from 2.1% to ~11.7% in early 2026; consumer pass-through is now estimated above 50%. This creates a supply-side inflation shock that the Taylor Rule was never designed for — the rule handles demand-driven cycles, not supply disruptions. The Fed's dilemma: hold rates to fight inflation and risk stalling growth, or cut to support employment and risk re-igniting prices. J.P. Morgan now sees the Fed's next move as a *hike* in Q3 2027, not a cut.

3. **Markets Price Zero Cuts in 2026 as Inflation Outlook Worsens** — *CME FedWatch / Yahoo Finance, April 2026*
   As of late April 2026, futures markets are pricing the majority probability on rates unchanged for the full year — a complete reversal from the three cuts priced at the start of 2025. The r* debate is central: if the neutral real rate has shifted from 0.5% to 1.5–2% (due to AI investment, fiscal deficits, deglobalization), then 3.5–3.75% may not be restrictive at all — meaning the hiking cycle may have accomplished less than it appeared.

### Devil's Advocate

The Taylor Rule is a regression fitted to a single peaceful decade of US economic data (1987–1992) that happened to work reasonably well for thirty years — until it didn't. The coefficients (0.5 on both the inflation gap and output gap) weren't derived from any economic theory; Taylor chose them because they described what Greenspan was already doing. Calling this a "rule" is generous. It's a description of one Fed chair's decisions, generalized into a formula and then treated as a universal law. When the economy is hit by supply shocks, pandemic distortions, or geopolitical tariff cascades — none of which appear in the formula — the rule has nothing to say except "raise rates," which is the monetary equivalent of applying the same solution to every problem.

The 2022–2024 "success" is the most dangerous piece of evidence for the Taylor Rule, because it may be entirely spurious. Inflation peaked in June 2022. The Fed's first hike was March 2022 — three months before the peak. With a 12–24 month transmission lag, the hikes that "caused" disinflation wouldn't have bitten until mid-2023 at the earliest. But inflation was already falling by then, driven by supply chain normalization, falling energy prices, and fiscal stimulus unwinding. Silicon Valley Bank failed in March 2023 precisely because the rate hikes had created massive duration risk in bank balance sheets. The Fed may have gotten credit for an outcome that happened despite its policy, while the costs — a near-banking-crisis, a housing market freeze, small business credit destruction — were entirely its own making.
