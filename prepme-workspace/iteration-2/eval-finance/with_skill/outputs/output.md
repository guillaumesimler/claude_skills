## Class Prep: Corporate Finance — WACC & Capital Structure

### Previous Session Summary

Last session built the theoretical spine of capital structure through the Modigliani-Miller framework and its real-world extensions.

**MM Proposition I (no taxes):** In perfect capital markets, firm value is independent of financing — `V_L = V_U`. Investors can replicate any leverage ratio through homemade leverage, so rearranging the right side of the balance sheet creates no value.

**MM Proposition II (no taxes):** As leverage rises, cost of equity rises proportionally — `r_E = r_U + (D/E)(r_U - r_D)` — exactly offsetting the cheaper debt. WACC stays flat. Equity becomes riskier as fixed debt obligations sit ahead of it.

**MM with taxes:** Interest is tax-deductible, giving debt a government subsidy: `V_L = V_U + T_c × D`. At 21% US corporate tax, every $1B of permanent debt adds ~$210M in value. WACC falls as leverage rises.

**Tradeoff theory:** Optimal capital structure balances the tax shield against financial distress costs: `V* = V_U + PV(Tax Shield) - PV(Distress Costs)`. Asset-heavy firms with stable cash flows (utilities, real estate) can carry more debt; high-growth, intangible-asset firms (tech, biotech) stay light because distress destroys option value.

**Tesla (2019–2023):** Near-bankruptcy in 2019 with expensive debt. As the stock surged in 2020–21, Tesla raised cheap equity multiple times, cutting leverage to near-zero. By 2023, with sustained GAAP profitability, it began moving toward a conventional investment-grade structure — a textbook tradeoff optimization as the distress cost calculus shifted.

### Devil's Advocate

The Modigliani-Miller theorem's core claim — that capital structure is irrelevant in perfect markets — is a tautology dressed as insight. Of course structure doesn't matter if you assume away every reason it matters: taxes, bankruptcy, information asymmetry, agency costs. What MM actually proved is that all of those things matter enormously, which is precisely why the assumptions had to be excluded to make the irrelevance result work. Using MM as a "baseline" is like using a frictionless physics model as a baseline for car braking distances — technically valid, operationally useless.

The tradeoff theory that replaced it isn't much better. Graham's 2000 study found that profitable firms systematically forgo 10% of their value by under-leveraging relative to what tradeoff theory prescribes. The theory predicts active rebalancing toward an optimal target; firms don't do it. Tesla "optimized" its capital structure opportunistically — issuing equity when the stock was at $800 because the market would buy it, not because some tradeoff calculation said it was time. Baker and Wurgler's market timing research (2002) shows capital structure is largely the residue of when firms got lucky with cheap financing, not deliberate optimization. The theory describes an equilibrium that no one is actually navigating toward.
