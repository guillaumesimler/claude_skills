## Class Prep: Strategy — Porter's Five Forces / Airline Industry

### Previous Session Summary

Last session introduced **Porter's Five Forces** as a framework for diagnosing industry-level competitive intensity and, by extension, predicting long-run profitability. The core logic: an industry's average profit margin is not random — it is shaped by five structural forces that determine how much value created gets captured by firms versus competed away to buyers, suppliers, and rivals.

Applied to **commercial aviation**, the analysis showed why the airline sector earns persistently thin margins despite enormous revenue volumes:

**1. Rivalry among existing competitors** is extreme. Airlines sell an undifferentiated commodity (a seat from A to B), compete on price through transparent booking platforms, face high fixed costs that incentivize capacity dumping, and have historically matched each other's fare cuts within hours.

**2. Threat of new entrants** is moderate-to-high over time. While aircraft, slots, and certifications create real barriers, LCCs like Ryanair, Spirit, and IndiGo have repeatedly proven the model is penetrable. New entrants routinely target profitable routes, eroding incumbent margins precisely where they existed.

**3. Bargaining power of buyers** is high. Individual travelers and corporate travel managers have near-perfect price transparency via aggregators. Switching costs are essentially zero. Price is the primary decision variable.

**4. Bargaining power of suppliers** is high. Boeing and Airbus constitute a duopoly. Jet fuel is a commodity priced by global markets. Pilot unions hold significant leverage, particularly during shortage periods.

**5. Threat of substitutes** varies by route but is real. High-speed rail competes on short-haul European and Asian routes. Videoconferencing substitutes for some business travel.

**Main takeaway:** No single force would be fatal alone, but all five hitting simultaneously creates a structural trap. Airlines are caught between high input costs they can't control and commoditized output prices they can't raise — which is why aggregate industry ROIC has historically lagged the cost of capital.

### In the News

*Note: WebSearch was unavailable in this session. Items below are drawn from well-documented developments through mid-2025.*

1. **Boeing's Production Crisis Squeezes Airline Capacity Plans** — *WSJ / Bloomberg, 2024–2025*
   Following the January 2024 Alaska Airlines door-plug blowout, the FAA capped 737 MAX production rates and delivery delays cascaded across the industry. Airlines including United, Southwest, and Ryanair were forced to pull capacity and revise growth plans. This is a live illustration of **supplier power** (Force 4): when a duopoly supplier stumbles, airlines have almost no alternative — Airbus also has a years-long backlog.

2. **Spirit Airlines Files for Bankruptcy** — *Reuters / FT, November 2024*
   Spirit filed for Chapter 11 in November 2024, the most prominent ULCC failure in recent years — driven by high fuel costs, aggressive pricing by legacy carriers, and the blocked merger with Frontier. This shows **new entrants** (Force 2) face genuine survival risk, but also that legacy carriers' competitive response to LCCs destroys value even for incumbents defending against entry.

3. **IATA Projects Record $36 Billion Industry Profit for 2024 — But Returns Still Below Cost of Capital** — *IATA / The Economist, June 2024*
   IATA forecast $36.4 billion in net profit for 2024 — a post-COVID high — yet the net margin was ~3.3% and ROIC still lagged WACC. This is the sharpest empirical anchor for the session's argument: even in a "record" year, airlines fail to create economic value by the standard financial definition.

### Devil's Advocate

**The Five Forces framework may overstate structural determinism and understate individual firm agency.**

Porter's analysis is compelling as an industry average — but averages obscure enormous variance. Southwest Airlines generated consistent profits for 47 consecutive years before COVID. Ryanair has maintained margins that would embarrass most manufacturers. Delta rebuilt itself from bankruptcy into a premium brand with loyalty economics that look nothing like commodity competition. If the structural trap were truly inescapable, these outcomes shouldn't exist.

The deeper critique: Porter's model is essentially **static** — it takes industry structure as given and asks how competitive that structure is. But strategists like Rita McGrath (transient advantage) and Clayton Christensen (disruption) would argue the interesting question is how firms *reshape* the structure rather than merely adapt to it. Southwest didn't just compete within the Five Forces — it redrew them by using point-to-point routing to avoid congested hubs, changing its supplier relationships, buyer expectations, and rivalry dynamics simultaneously.

There's also a **measurement problem**: ROIC averaged across the industry includes dozens of failing carriers that drag down the mean. A more surgical cut might find that the top quartile — firms with strong network effects, loyalty programs, or geographic monopolies on thin routes — consistently earn above their cost of capital.

The takeaway: Five Forces is a powerful diagnostic, but stopping at "the industry is unattractive" is half the work. The more interesting strategy question is: **given this structure, what positions or moves allow a firm to escape the average?**
