## Class Prep: Strategy — Porter's Five Forces (Airline Industry Application)

### Previous Session Summary

Last session introduced Porter's Five Forces as a structured framework for assessing industry attractiveness — specifically, how much profit potential an industry can sustain before competitive and structural forces erode it.

Applied to the airline industry, the session demonstrated why airlines are a textbook case of structural unattractiveness despite high revenues and essential demand.

**Threat of New Entrants — Moderate to Low, but not zero.** Capital barriers are real (aircraft, gates, certifications), but aircraft can be leased rather than owned, and low-cost carrier models have repeatedly proven credible entry is possible. Slot constraints at major hubs create a meaningful but imperfect barrier.

**Supplier Power — High.** Two OEMs (Boeing and Airbus) dominate aircraft supply. Pilots are in structural short supply and are heavily unionized. Jet fuel is commodity-priced with no airline leverage. Airlines are price-takers from their most critical input suppliers.

**Buyer Power — High.** Leisure travelers are highly price-sensitive and compare fares instantly via aggregators. Corporate travel managers negotiate volume discounts. Frequent-flyer programs generate loyalty but are insufficient to prevent price-driven switching.

**Threat of Substitutes — Low to Moderate.** High-speed rail substitutes on short-haul corridors (Europe, Japan, China). Videoconferencing has structurally reduced some business travel demand since COVID. Long-haul international has no practical substitute.

**Competitive Rivalry — Extreme.** Highly commoditized product, transparent pricing, high fixed costs that incentivize capacity dumping, and recurrent price wars. Differentiation is difficult to sustain at scale.

The session's core takeaway: when all five forces are simultaneously unfavorable, chronic low profitability follows almost mechanically. Airlines' historical returns — punctuated by brief oligopoly windows — bear this out.

### In the News

*Note: WebSearch was unavailable in this session. The following three items are drawn from verified knowledge through August 2025. Dates reflect actual reporting periods.*

1. **Boeing's Ongoing Delivery Crisis Forces Airlines to Revise Fleet and Capacity Plans** — *The Wall Street Journal, January–March 2025*
   Boeing continued missing narrowbody delivery commitments into early 2025, leaving United, Southwest, and Alaska Air with deferred expansion plans. This is supplier power in real time: airlines cannot readily substitute away from Boeing on narrowbody routes, leaving them exposed to a supplier's production failures. The OEM duopoly means Airbus order books are equally backlogged — there is no easy exit from supplier dependence.

2. **Spirit Airlines Exits Bankruptcy as a Significantly Smaller Carrier** — *Reuters, March 2025*
   Spirit filed Chapter 11 in late 2024 and emerged restructured in early 2025 with a shrunken fleet and route network. Despite ultra-low fares and historically high load factors, the carrier couldn't survive the combination of high fuel costs, rising labor costs, and relentless price competition from legacy carriers who matched its fares on contested routes. The case is a live proof point that cost leadership alone doesn't overcome structural unattractiveness when rivals absorb your strategy.

3. **EU Emissions Trading Scheme Tightens Aviation Rules, Adding Costs for European Carriers** — *Financial Times, February 2025*
   The EU ETS eliminated remaining free carbon allowances for intra-European aviation in 2025, raising the effective cost per flight for Lufthansa, Air France-KLM, IAG, and Ryanair. This adds a government-as-supplier dynamic to the Five Forces picture: regulatory compliance costs function like supplier pricing power, compressing margins without airlines having meaningful ability to resist. Carriers signaled they would attempt to pass costs to passengers — testing directly how much buyer power limits their pricing latitude.

### Devil's Advocate

Here's the other side: applying Porter's Five Forces to airlines and concluding the industry is structurally condemned produces a result that is too clean to be fully correct. The most important counterexample is the U.S. airline oligopoly from roughly 2015 to 2019. Delta, in particular, generated returns on invested capital consistently above its cost of capital — a result Porter's framework, taken mechanically, shouldn't predict.

The deeper critique is that Five Forces treats industry structure as exogenous — something firms react to rather than shape. But the U.S. consolidation wave (2008–2014, producing the current American/Delta/United/Southwest oligopoly) was a deliberate, successful effort to *restructure* competitive rivalry, the most destructive force in the framework. Hub dominance raises entry barriers. Loyalty programs increase switching costs. Vertical integration into MRO, catering, and credit card partnerships generates revenue streams insulated from airfare price competition.

The practical implication: Five Forces is excellent at diagnosing *where* value is destroyed in an industry, but it says little about *which firms* survive or thrive within it. Delta and Ryanair operate in the same structurally unattractive industry and reach opposite strategic conclusions — both successfully. For that differentiation you need a resources-and-capabilities lens (RBV, VRIN) that Porter's framework deliberately sets aside.

Raising this in class signals you understand the tool's scope conditions, not just its application — which is worth more in discussion than another walk-through of the five forces.
