## Class Prep: Macroeconomics — Session on Monetary Policy & the Fed

### Previous Session Summary

Last session covered how central banks set interest rates using systematic frameworks, with the 2022–2024 Fed hiking cycle as the primary case study.

**The Taylor Rule** (John Taylor, 1993) is a formula for deriving the appropriate policy rate: `i = r* + π + 0.5(π - π*) + 0.5(y - y*)`. It says the nominal rate should equal the neutral real rate (r*) plus current inflation, plus adjustments for the inflation gap (how far inflation is from the 2% target) and the output gap (how far the economy is from potential). The rule prescribes higher rates when inflation exceeds target *and* when the economy runs hot — and lower rates in the opposite conditions.

**The 2021 miss:** The Taylor Rule was prescribing rates above 4% as early as mid-2021. The Fed didn't start hiking until March 2022, calling inflation "transitory." This was a forecast error — not a model error. The rule was right; the inflation inputs were wrong.

**The 2022–2024 cycle:** The Fed hiked 525 basis points in 16 months — the fastest cycle since Volcker's 1979–1982 campaign. CPI peaked at 9.1% in June 2022. The Fed reached 5.25–5.50% by July 2023, held there through mid-2024, then began cutting in September 2024. By end-2024, the target range was 4.25–4.50%.

**The near-soft landing:** By most measures, the cycle achieved something historically rare — inflation fell from 9.1% to ~3% without a recession. GDP growth stayed positive. Unemployment rose only from ~3.4% to ~4.1%. The debate: was this skill (credible forward guidance), luck (supply chains self-healing), or a delayed reckoning still to come?

**Transmission mechanism:** Higher FFR → higher borrowing costs across mortgages, corporate bonds, car loans → reduced spending and investment → lower aggregate demand → lower inflation. The lag is 12–24 months, which is why the Fed has to act preemptively.

The Fed's impossible dilemma: hold rates (risk stalling growth) or cut (risk re-igniting inflation).

### Devil's Advocate

Here's the challenge to the class's framework: **the Taylor Rule is a reasonable model for demand-driven inflation cycles, but the 2022–2024 episode may have been primarily supply-driven — which means the rate hikes were the wrong tool, and we got lucky.**

The textbook Taylor Rule story assumes that raising rates reduces inflation by cooling aggregate demand. But the 2022 inflation spike had significant supply-side components: COVID-era supply chain disruptions, energy shocks from the Ukraine war, and pandemic-era labor market dislocations. These don't respond to interest rates — they resolve on their own timeline.

If that's true, inflation would have come down in 2023–2024 *regardless of* rate hikes, as supply chains normalized and energy prices stabilized. The soft landing wasn't engineered — it was inherited. The Fed raised rates aggressively, nearly broke the banking system (Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March 2023 directly because of duration risk created by rapid rate hikes), and caused enormous pain in housing and small business credit — and then got credit for an outcome that would have happened anyway.

The deeper implication: the Taylor Rule gives the Fed a scientific veneer of precision it may not deserve. The rule's two key inputs — r* (the neutral rate) and the output gap — are both unobservable and heavily revised ex-post. Policymakers are fitting a formula to data they can't measure well, in an economy where the transmission lags mean they're always reacting to last year's conditions.

**For class:** Ask whether the Taylor Rule is a *decision tool* or a *communication tool* — a way of explaining decisions already made on other grounds.
