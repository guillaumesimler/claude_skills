**Session focus:** Taylor Rule, central bank rate-setting, and the 2022–2024 rate hiking cycle

## 1. Core Concepts You Must Own Cold

### The Federal Reserve: Structure & Mandate

- **Dual mandate:** Maximum employment + price stability (2% inflation target)
- **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC):** 12 voting members; sets the federal funds rate (FFR) — the overnight rate banks charge each other
- **Policy tools:** Open market operations (primary), discount rate, reserve requirements, interest on reserve balances (IORB), reverse repos (RRP)
- The FFR is a *target range*, not a single number (e.g., "5.25%–5.50%")

### The Taylor Rule — The Formula Behind Rate Decisions

The Taylor Rule (John Taylor, 1993) gives a formulaic prescription for where the policy rate *should* be:

```
i = r* + π + 0.5(π - π*) + 0.5(y - y*)
```

Where:
- `i` = nominal policy rate
- `r*` = neutral real interest rate (~0.5%–1% historically; now debated at 1%–2%)
- `π` = current inflation rate
- `π*` = inflation target (2%)
- `y - y*` = output gap (actual GDP vs. potential GDP)

**Key intuition:** If inflation > 2%, the rule prescribes a higher rate. If output < potential, it prescribes a lower rate. Why did the Taylor Rule signal hikes *earlier* than the Fed acted in 2021? Because the Fed incorrectly assumed inflation was "transitory."

### Transmission Mechanism

```
Fed raises FFR → Higher borrowing costs → Less spending/investment → Lower aggregate demand → Lower inflation (12–24 month lag)
```

A higher FFR also strengthens the dollar, making imports cheaper (disinflationary).

## 2. The 2022–2024 Hiking Cycle: The Full Story

| Period | What Happened |
|--------|--------------|
| 2021 | CPI hit 7%; Fed called it "transitory"; Taylor Rule already prescribing 5%+ |
| Mar 2022 | Liftoff: first hike (+25 bps) |
| Jun–Nov 2022 | Four consecutive 75 bps "jumbo" hikes; CPI peaked at 9.1% in June |
| Jul 2023 | Peak: 5.25%–5.50% (525 bps total in 16 months) |
| Aug 2023–Aug 2024 | Holding at peak ("higher for longer") |
| Sep 2024 | First cut: 50 bps; cutting cycle begins |
| End 2024 | FFR at 4.25%–4.50% |

This was the **fastest hiking cycle since Volcker** (1979–1981).

---

## 3. Key Debates

**Soft landing vs. hard landing:** The 2022–2024 cycle produced a near-soft landing — GDP stayed positive, unemployment only rose from ~3.4% to ~4.1%, inflation fell from 9.1% to ~3%. Historically rare.

**The r* debate:** If the neutral real rate has risen from 0.5% to 1.5%–2% (due to AI investment, deglobalization, fiscal deficits), then "5% might not be that restrictive" — and future neutral rates will be higher than the 2010s.

**The tariff shock (2025):** Trump's April 2025 tariffs (145% on Chinese goods) created a supply-side inflation risk. The Fed faces a stagflation dilemma: rate hikes don't fix supply shocks; they just add recession risk.

## 4. Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| FFR | Overnight interbank rate; Fed's primary tool |
| Basis point | 1/100th of 1% |
| Dual mandate | Price stability + maximum employment |
| Core PCE | Fed's preferred inflation gauge (ex-food/energy) |
| r* | Neutral real rate — neither stimulates nor restricts |
| Output gap | Actual minus potential GDP |
| Terminal rate | Peak rate in a hiking cycle (here: 5.25%–5.50%) |
| Soft landing | Inflation controlled without recession |
| Stagflation | High inflation + stagnant growth |
| QT | Quantitative tightening — shrinking the Fed's balance sheet |

## 5. Devil's Advocate Challenges

- The Taylor Rule's coefficients (0.5, 0.5) are arbitrary — fitted to 1987–1992 data, may not apply to supply-shock inflation
- SVB's March 2023 collapse was directly caused by rapid rate hikes — the Fed may have created financial stability risk while fighting inflation
- The "transitory" call may have reflected political economy pressures, not just economic analysis
